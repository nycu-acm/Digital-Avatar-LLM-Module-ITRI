Innovation technology, progress in renewable energy, and data center power demand have become the focus of attention across the United States. Lincoln, Nebraska lets all walks of life see that it is actually the local power company that shoulders the heavy responsibility of energy transformation. One morning in mid-March, a spring blizzard was stuck in the sky above eastern Nebraska. It was moving slowly and violently blew the capital Lincoln. The strong winds at speeds of up to 60 miles per hour were accompanied by rain and snow, with a maximum snowfall of 8 inches. The local power company Lincoln Electric System has about 150,000 users, and nearly 10% of them were in a power outage at noon. The weight of ice and snow accumulated on the wires, slapped each other, causing the circuit to be locked. Strong winds continued, and Lincoln Airport even measured the wind speed as high as 74 miles at one point, causing a row of telephone poles to break in the northern edge of the city and fall on the open fields. An Yanwu (Emeka) Anyanwu opened the power outage distribution map on the screen, and reorganized it almost every 10 minutes. At the same time, 18 maintenance teams were dispatched on site, with a total of about 75 to 80 line engineers working hard to repair it, trying to narrow the orange circles on the screen, each circle representing thousands of power outage users. But for him, this spring blizzard was just a small question Question. Although these storms are increasingly frequent, they are predictable interference events after all. The test will probably be even more severe in the near future. It is a hard battle for Lincoln Power or all power operators. The power industry calls this future challenge a "triple dilemma": how to balance the stable power supply, affordable price and sustainable development has become a nearly unsolvable problem. As winds and fires become more extreme and frequent, the risks of cyber attacks and physical facilities are increasing, and the attitudes of policies and regulations are highly unclear, power companies must continue to maintain The power supply is not interrupted, and the electricity price must be lowered due to inflation costs. In addition, power companies must keep pace with the times, because the operating model of the power grid has completely changed, and the power generation source has gradually transformed from fossil fuels to renewable energy such as solar and wind. However, the triple dilemma has become a basic threshold in the past year, and other such as technological progress and economic considerations have gradually formed strong pressure, making future interference almost a foregone conclusion. In the near future, the power grid will not only face irresistible changes, but also face existing limitations that are difficult to shake, and many factors are also the same. This is a concern, but it is opposite to each other. Because of this, An Yanwu's calm and rational response attitude allows all walks of life to use Lincoln Electric to effectively examine the challenges that the power grid is about to face. The storm is becoming more and more intense. For power companies, the most urgent technical challenge is the rising demand for electricity, which is partly related to the progress of artificial intelligence. Looking back at the history of the power industry, population growth brings power consumption growth every time, while energy efficiency continues to improve (mainly the contribution of LED lighting and home appliance technology advancement), so energy saving silently offsets the increase in electricity consumption. The balance no longer exists now. In addition to the construction of new data centers and factories, the electrification of vehicles, kitchens and home heating has also increased electricity demand and broken the long-term balance model. Since 2000, the annual growth rate of electricity load has always been lower than 1%, but is now expected to exceed 3%. In 2022, the power grid originally estimated that the capacity of new devices will reach 23 gigawatts (GW) in the next five years, but now it is expected to increase by another 128 gigawatts. As for the political test, I believe the whole world is familiar with it, that is, Trump's administrative style of chaos in the world. During the Biden administration A number of major bills have been introduced in the period, which has led dozens of industries to adopt renewable energy. Thanks to large-scale tax incentives, clean technology manufacturing and renewable energy development have flourished; government policies encourage wind and solar energy to build facilities on federal land; next-generation energy technologies such as energy storage, nuclear energy and geothermal heat can also obtain live water of funds. Now the Trump administration’s route has taken a sharp turn, and climate-related policies are the most obvious. On the one hand, the government slows down and even suspends the approval of offshore and onshore wind power facilities, and on the other hand, it issues administrative orders to encourage the development of coal and other Fossil fuels (but this move will inevitably lead to legal lawsuits). The Trump administration declared the United States to enter an "energy emergency", which may seriously disrupt the intricate regulatory structure of the power grid and subvert the rules of the game for power companies. Trump's incitement remarks alone are enough to boost some communities' courage to strengthen opposition to new wind and solar projects, causing developers to face higher costs and variables, and even cause plans to be undeveloped. Despite this, the momentum of energy transformation is still strong and even beyond control. According to the Energy Information Association (Energy) Information Administration predicted in 2025 that the capacity of public-scale power generation will increase by 63 GW, of which 93% come from solar, wind or energy storage. Take Texas as an example, about 92% of the waiting list for grid-connected Internet (a leading indicator for power plant construction project) is solar, wind and energy storage systems.How the power industry will develop in the future is both obvious but difficult to predict. The current situation is chaotic and intertwined with many large forces, and there is also a political storm to disrupt the situation, which is like adding another dilemma in the triple dilemma. What the new technology level really has is a series of difficult problems. For Lincoln Power, the most difficult problem emerged in April 2024, that is, how to increase power generation capacity without increasing costs or carbon emissions. Like most power companies in the United States, Lincoln Power must rely on an independent "regional transmission organization" (RTO), which balances power supply and demand, ensures stable power supply in the power grid, and operates power trading markets. This system The operating principle is that when all power companies share the power consumption and power generation of the power grid, they can benefit from it, which can achieve stable power supply and economic efficiency. "Think of the market as everyone prepares a dish for dinner." An Yanwu said: "Everyone should bring enough food to eat, but it does not mean that they have to eat the one they bring." In other words, every power company must contribute enough power generation capacity according to its peak electricity needs, but all power will be shared for the whole System use. The larger the grid, the more it can absorb small fluctuations or sudden failures. But now, people who eat together are getting hungry, but the oven may not be operating normally. In the past, the only variable of a power plant was "on or off", and judging the power generation capacity was relatively simple. A 164 million-watt natural gas or coal-fired power plant can usually provide the same power generation stably. Wind power and solar energy have no fuel costs or carbon emissions, but they break this model. The industry calls "resource adequacy" Adequacy involves complex averages and estimates, both calculated based on peak electricity load peaks in peak seasons of power companies. When encountering record-breaking power peaks, all power plants must be powered online at the same time. However, wind and solar energy cannot be opened as soon as possible. Summer peak days may be cloudy or windless, and winter peak days must be when the sun sets early. Coal and natural gas power plants are not without challenges of stable power supply. Such power plants are often shut down due to maintenance. In winter, underground pipelines that transport natural gas may also freeze, making it difficult to cope with the huge demands of home heating and large power plants at the same time. Against the background of rapid changes in the composition of power generation resources, the Southwest Power Alliance, a regional power transmission organization responsible for most of the United States (including Nebraska) Pool) decided to be cautious, so in August 2024, the "capacity certification" standard is revised, that is, how much electricity is expected to contribute stably during peak periods. All power sources are scored based on the reliability curve. If natural gas power plants often fail, the certification capacity will be relatively low; if there is a large amount of wind power, the certification capacity will be higher during peak periods in winter (stronger winds) than in summer; solar power plants have higher certification capacity in summer than in winter because summer The sun is long and sufficient. According to the new formula designed by the Southwest Electric Power Alliance, Lincoln Electric must contribute more electricity to the grid, just like someone who believes that a pound of burger meat can feed more than a pound of tofu. The new system is obviously biased towards fossil fuel generation, punishing wind and solar energy, and is therefore sneered by the Clean Power and Environmental Protection Initiative Group. (Institutional adjustments are the result of industrial lobbying, deep-rooted ideology, or insufficient technical understanding, and are still unknown.) However, the "resource adequacy" itself is difficult to refute, and no one dares to underestimate the problem of instability in power supply. Judging from the triple dilemma of the power industry, this is like a stick holding a "reliability" and playing a horse with "affordability", while "sustainability" can only wait to appear on the side. Political factors are no longer the focus, and maintaining stable power supply (including in response to the electricity demand of artificial intelligence data centers) has become a new goal.
